WHY LOGISTICS WORKERS ARE ORGANIZING AGAIN

The Quiet Resurgence of the Warehouse Union

By Helena Stein
Published in "Mercados & Tendencias" magazine, March 2024 issue
Industry & Labor section, pages 34-39

A decade after the wave of anti-union campaigns that followed the
2008 financial crisis, warehouse workers in the United States, the
United Kingdom, Germany, and Spain are organizing again at a pace
not seen since the late 1990s. In the twelve months ending January
2024, petitions for union representation filed with the relevant
labor boards increased 38% in the United States and 21% in the
United Kingdom, according to data compiled by the Worker Institute
at Cornell University and by the Trades Union Congress.

The reasons are not hard to find. The accelerated automation of
loading and sorting operations has eliminated many of the lower-
skilled manual positions that historically served as a first job in
the sector, while creating a smaller workforce of technically
qualified technicians. At the same time, the prevalence of just-in-
time scheduling, performance quotas enforced by handheld scanners,
and the outsourcing of last-mile delivery to subcontractors have
increased the day-to-day uncertainty faced by the workers who
remain.

"In our last three quarterly engagement surveys, the two issues
that consistently scored lowest were scheduling predictability and
the perceived fairness of the discipline process," said a senior
human resources director at a major European third-party logistics
provider, who agreed to speak on condition of anonymity. "Workers
no longer interpret these as isolated frustrations. They read them
as structural, and they want collective answers to collective
problems."

Industry analysts caution against reading the recent uptick as a
return to the mass unionization of the post-war decades. Union
density in the logistics sector remains below 9% in most OECD
economies, well under the 22% average for the private sector as a
whole. The current wave is concentrated in facilities with more
than 500 workers, in regions with a pre-existing union tradition,
and in companies whose workforce skews younger than the national
median.

Even so, the implications for logistics operators are significant.
A 2023 study by the OECD estimated that the direct cost of a
two-week work stoppage in a mid-sized automated warehouse ranges
between USD 4.2 million and USD 11 million, depending on the share
of throughput that can be diverted to alternative sites.

Operators with no union presence today are unlikely to face an
organizing campaign in the short term, but the warning signs are
clear: predictability of hours, transparency of the discipline
process, and a credible channel for grievances to be heard
collectively rather than individually.

For an industry that has spent the last fifteen years optimizing
for throughput, the next competitive advantage may turn out to be
the quality of the working environment.

Helena Stein is a contributing editor at Mercados & Tendencias and
the author of "The Algorithm and the Loading Bay" (Editorial
Planeta, 2022).
